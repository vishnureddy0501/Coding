# Saga Design Pattern — Interview Notes

## What Is the Saga Pattern?

Each service completes its own local database transaction. If a later step fails, previously completed steps are undone using **compensating transactions**.

> **Simple definition:** A Saga breaks one large distributed transaction into smaller local transactions and compensates for completed steps when something fails.

## Why Is It Needed?

Consider placing an order:

1. Order Service creates the order.
2. Payment Service charges the customer.
3. Inventory Service reserves the product.
4. Shipping Service schedules delivery.

Each service owns a separate database. A normal database transaction cannot easily cover all of them.

Without a Saga, payment might succeed while inventory reservation fails, leaving the system inconsistent.

## Successful Flow

```text
Create Order
     ↓
Charge Payment
     ↓
Reserve Inventory
     ↓
Arrange Shipping
     ↓
Order Confirmed
```

Each step commits its own local transaction.

## Failure and Compensation

Suppose inventory reservation fails after payment succeeds:

```text
Create Order       ✓
Charge Payment     ✓
Reserve Inventory  ✗
```

The Saga runs compensating actions:

```text
Refund Payment
      ↓
Cancel Order
```

A compensation is a new business operation, not a database rollback. For example, refunding a payment reverses its business effect but does not erase the original charge from history.

## Two Ways to Implement a Saga

### 1. Choreography

Services communicate by publishing and reacting to events. There is no central coordinator.

```text
Order Service
  │ publishes OrderCreated
  ▼
Payment Service
  │ publishes PaymentCompleted
  ▼
Inventory Service
  │ publishes InventoryReserved
  ▼
Shipping Service
```

If inventory reservation fails, it publishes `InventoryReservationFailed`. Payment Service refunds the payment, and Order Service cancels the order.

#### Advantages

- No central coordinator
- Services remain loosely coupled
- Suitable for simple workflows with few steps

#### Drawbacks

- The complete workflow is difficult to understand.
- Many event dependencies can form a confusing chain.
- Monitoring and debugging become harder.
- Adding more steps increases complexity quickly.

### 2. Orchestration

A central Saga orchestrator tells each service what to do and tracks the workflow state.

```text
                Saga Orchestrator
                /       |       \
               ▼        ▼        ▼
          Payment   Inventory   Shipping
           Service    Service    Service
```

The orchestrator:

1. Requests payment.
2. Waits for the result.
3. Requests inventory reservation.
4. Requests shipping.
5. Runs compensations in reverse order if a step fails.

#### Advantages

- The workflow is visible in one place.
- Failure handling is easier to understand.
- Suitable for long or complex business processes.

#### Drawbacks

- The orchestrator is an additional component to operate.
- It can become too large if it contains service-specific business logic.
- Services become dependent on the orchestration protocol.

## Choreography Versus Orchestration

| Choreography | Orchestration |
| --- | --- |
| Services react to events | A coordinator controls the flow |
| No central controller | Central workflow state |
| Good for simple flows | Good for complex flows |
| Harder to see the full process | Easier to monitor and debug |

## Important Design Considerations

### Idempotency

A step or compensation may be requested more than once because of retries. Operations such as charging, refunding, and reserving inventory must be idempotent.

### Retries and Timeouts

Retry temporary failures using limited attempts and backoff. Every remote step needs a timeout so the Saga does not wait forever.

### Saga State

Track the current step, completed steps, failures, retries, and compensation status. This state must survive an orchestrator restart.

### Eventual Consistency

A Saga does not make all services update at the same instant. The system moves through temporary states before eventually becoming consistent.

For example, an order may remain in `PAYMENT_COMPLETED` while inventory reservation is still pending.

### Compensation Can Fail

A refund or inventory release can also fail. Compensations need retries, monitoring, alerts, and sometimes manual intervention.

## When to Use It

Use the Saga pattern when:

- A transaction spans multiple services and databases.
- A distributed ACID transaction is unavailable or undesirable.
- Business actions can be compensated.
- Eventual consistency is acceptable.

Avoid it when:

- A single local database transaction is sufficient.
- The operation requires immediate, strict consistency across all steps.
- A completed action cannot be compensated safely.

## Common Interview Questions

### Is compensation the same as rollback?

No. A rollback erases an uncommitted database change. A compensation is a new action that reverses the business effect of an already committed action.

### What if a compensation fails?

Retry it safely, record its status, alert operators, and provide a manual recovery process. Never assume compensation always succeeds.

### How do you prevent duplicate payment or refund requests?

Use a stable idempotency key for every Saga step and compensation.

### How do you monitor a Saga?

Store a Saga ID and correlation ID across all requests and events. Track each step, duration, retry, failure, and compensation in logs and metrics.

### Should you use choreography or orchestration?

Use choreography for short and simple event flows. Prefer orchestration when the workflow has many steps, branches, timeouts, or complex compensation logic.

### Does Saga provide strong consistency?

No. A Saga provides eventual consistency through local transactions and compensating actions.

## Interview Answer

> The Saga pattern manages a transaction across multiple services by splitting it into local transactions. Each successful step triggers the next one. If a later step fails, compensating transactions reverse the business effects of completed steps. Sagas can use choreography through events or orchestration through a central coordinator. The main challenges are eventual consistency, idempotency, failed compensations, and monitoring the workflow.

## Quick Revision

```text
Local transactions + compensating actions = Saga
```

Remember:

1. Each service commits its own transaction.
2. There is no single transaction across every database.
3. Failures trigger compensating business operations.
4. Choreography uses events; orchestration uses a coordinator.
5. Every step and compensation should be idempotent.
6. Compensations can fail and need recovery handling.
7. Sagas provide eventual consistency, not immediate consistency.
